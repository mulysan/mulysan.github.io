Who keeps trading, and how traders behave when every position is public

Shmuel San · October 2026

On Polymarket every trade is public and every contract ends worth exactly $1 or nothing. That makes it possible to answer questions about traders that stock-market data leave open. Does luck decide who keeps trading? Does money drift toward the skilled? Do traders sell winners too early and hold losers too long, and does it cost them? This page follows 2.1 million wallets across 74,000 resolved markets. It is the fourth in a series, after how much prices know, who wins and the Israeli election markets.

What the data show

  1. Luck decides who keeps trading. In 4,448 markets that were a near coin flip a day before they resolved (priced 40–60¢), some wallets happened to win and others to lose. In regressions with wallet and cohort-by-week fixed effects, the two groups traded alike in the weeks before. Afterwards the winners trade in 3 more weeks out of every 100 (16% more than the losers) and buy about 40% more dollars a week, for at least 11 weeks. For wallets whose very first bet was such a coin flip, the effect is bigger: winners trade in 5–7 more weeks out of every 100, about half as often again as losers. Winning makes them trade more, not better: their profits on other markets do not rise.
  2. Money follows recent results, not skill. Wallets that made money on a quarter's markets are 10–15 points more likely to trade the next quarter than those that lost, and their share of the money rises from 60% to 69%. But since returns barely persist (traders page) and luck alone moves people this much, this selects on luck. Wallets with a proven record supply only 3–4% of the money each quarter, with no upward trend, while wallets with little or no record supply a quarter to two-fifths.
  3. No learning by doing. Following 13,054 wallets over their first 100 markets, their return per market does not improve with experience: it stays within half a percentage point of each wallet's own average.
  4. Traders sell winners and hold losers, but here it does not hurt them. When a position is at a gain, wallets sell at 50% of their trading moments; at a loss, at 40%. The gap is larger for bigger wallets, about 15 points. Yet the shares they sell at a gain end up worth only about 1¢ more than the losing shares they keep, and the wallets most prone to it earn more than those least prone (+2.3% against −1.9% per dollar). Prices on Polymarket partly reverse after moves (calibration page), so taking gains after a rise is not a costly habit here.
  5. After losses, a small turn to long shots. In the week after a large loss, wallets put 4–5 more points of their buying into contracts under 10¢. After a large win the shift is about half that. In the clean coin-flip comparison, unlucky losers shift toward long shots by only 0.6 points more than lucky winners: a weak "break-even" effect and no "house money" effect.
  6. Long-shot lovers lose a quarter of what they spend. Wallets putting half or more of their money into contracts under 10¢ lost 25% per dollar; those that rarely buy long shots roughly broke even. Long-shot contracts are priced about right on average, so the losses more likely come from how they are bought, for instance paying a wide spread on cheap contracts in thin markets.

1 · Lucky winners keep trading: evidence from coin flips

Whether a trader keeps going after a win or a loss mixes two things: winners may be better traders, and better traders stay. To separate them, use bets whose outcome was close to random. A market priced between 40¢ and 60¢ a day before it resolved was nearly a coin flip as far as the market could tell. The wallets that held one side won or lost largely by luck. For each wallet, take its first such market and follow how often it trades in each week, from 8 weeks before to 12 weeks after.

Before the event the two groups are indistinguishable week by week: the difference never exceeds one percentage point. There are more losers than winners, because more wallets held the side that lost, and the losers were somewhat smaller wallets (median buying over the previous 8 weeks of $401 against $594). But they traded at the same rate. After the event the paths split at once and stay apart for the whole 12 weeks. The winners trade about 3.7 points more often each week, roughly a fifth more than the losers, and fewer of them quit altogether. The gap is there too when the stake was under a quarter of the wallet's recent buying (4 points a week on a higher base), so it is not simply that winners had more money to trade with. Traders respond to outcomes they could not control, much like investors who keep buying IPOs after lucky first experiences (Kaustia and Knüpfer, 2008).

The formal version. The regressions follow each wallet week by week, from 8 weeks before its coin-flip market resolved to 11 weeks after. They include a fixed effect for each wallet, which absorbs how active it is in general, and one for each cohort-by-week cell. A cohort is the calendar week the coin-flip market resolved, so winners are compared with losers whose market resolved the same week, at the same distance from the event. The coefficient on winning in each week traces the effect over time. The pooled version compares weeks +1 to +11 with weeks −8 to −2. Week −1 often contains the purchase of the coin-flip position itself, and in week 0 other markets resolving the same day carry a mechanical share of the result, so both are left out.

The weeks before are flat: relative to their own average, the coefficients for weeks −8 to −2 stay within about half a percentage point. Week −1 dips, because wallets that bought late, in the final week, ended up on the losing side more often. Then activity jumps: winners are 5.5 points more likely than losers to trade in the week after the market resolves, and about 2.5 points more likely in each of the following ten weeks. Pooled over weeks +1 to +11, winning raises the chance of trading in a week by 3.0 points against a loser rate of 18%. It raises dollars bought by about 0.2 log points ($105 a week against $261), dollars sold by 0.12 and the number of trades by 7%. The narrower 45–55¢ window gives larger effects (4.2 points, 0.34 log points) and the wider 30–70¢ window slightly smaller ones. Comparing only wallets that bet on the same side, Yes or No, changes nothing.

Two things do not change. Winners' profits on markets in other events are no higher in weeks +1 to +11: +$0.36 a week, not distinguishable from zero (in the 45–55¢ window, whose wallets are larger and profitable on average, +$5.70, about what trading more at the same return would give). All of the apparent profit gain in the 40–60¢ window sits in week 0, when related events resolve together. And their buying shifts only slightly toward favourites: the long-shot share of their purchases is about 1.4 points lower than the losers', and the average price they pay barely moves. Luck changes how much people trade, not how well.

First bets. A wallet whose very first market was a coin flip has no history, so there are no "weeks before" to compare, but also no record of skill that could differ between winners and losers. Of 122,538 such wallets in the 40–60¢ window, those that happened to win trade in about 5 more weeks out of every 100 over the next 11 weeks than those that lost, from a loser rate of 10%. That is about half as often again, three times the effect for experienced wallets. They also buy about $10 a week more against $20. Winners had, on average, bet on the side priced a little higher (54¢ against 48¢), as favourites win slightly more often. Comparing only wallets that bet on the same side, and holding that side's price fixed, makes the effect larger still: 7 points. In the narrow 45–55¢ window first-time winners trade about 80% more often than first-time losers.

First-time losers also turn to long shots: in the weeks they trade, 3 to 5 points more of their buying goes into contracts under 10¢ than winners'. They do not win back their losses: neither group's later profits differ by much, and first-time winners, who trade more, lose slightly more in total ($0.09 a week). A newcomer's first result, which is mostly luck, shapes how much they trade for months.

Does the size of the bet matter? Splitting the experienced wallets into ten equal groups by the dollar value of the position, and estimating the same difference-in-differences coefficient in each, shows that for experienced wallets the effect grows with the stake. For bets between $50 and $400 winning raises the chance of trading in a week by 10–25% of the losers' rate. For bets around $800 it is 38%, and for bets of several thousand dollars 46%. Across the ten groups the effect rises by about 19% of the losers' rate for each tenfold increase in the bet (standard error 4.5; correlation 0.81), and dollars bought by 0.13 log points per tenfold.

Bet size here partly measures the wallet: big bets come from big traders. Measuring the bet against the wallet's own buying over the previous 8 weeks separates the two. The effect is smallest when the coin flip was a trivial share of the wallet's trading (8% when it was 1%). It rises to about 30% when the bet was a tenth to a third of recent buying, and to 47–65% when it was about as large as everything the wallet had bought in those 8 weeks. Beyond that the pattern breaks up: the few wallets that bet more than their recent buying respond less, and noisily. So bigger stakes, relative to the trader, produce bigger responses, though not in a straight line.

2 · Does money flow to skilled traders?

If markets select for skill, money should drift from unskilled to skilled traders over time and prices should get sharper as it does. The first half of that happens on Polymarket, but on the wrong signal. Wallets that made money on the markets resolving in a quarter are more likely to trade in the next one. Among wallets that spent at least $10,000, 57% of winners trade again against 42% of losers. The winners' share of the money rises from 60% to 69%. Returns, however, barely persist from one period to the next (traders page), and the coin flips show that luck alone moves people this much.

Measured by record rather than by last quarter's luck, there is no drift toward skill. Each quarter, wallets with a statistically proven record supply 3–4% of the money, proven losers 4–10%, and wallets with little or no record between a quarter and two-fifths. Prices did get sharper over 2025–26 (Brier skill a day out rose from 0.44 to 0.63 between early 2025 and mid-2026), but not because skilled capital took over. And individual traders do not get better with experience: following the same wallets over their first 100 markets, their returns are flat.

3 · Selling winners, holding losers

The disposition effect, investors' readiness to sell winners and reluctance to sell losers, is one of the best-documented biases in finance (Shefrin and Statman, 1985; Odean, 1998). Measuring it needs each investor's purchase price and, to say whether it costs anything, what the shares were eventually worth. Polymarket gives both exactly. Every order by a wallet that already holds the outcome it trades is a moment at which it could sell. The position is at a gain or a loss by comparing the price with the wallet's average purchase price.

The effect is clear: wallets sell at 50% of the moments when they are ahead and 40% when they are behind. It is weak among the smallest wallets and about 15 points among those that traded $10k–1m, and 58% of wallets with enough events show it. But it costs little. Across wallets, shares sold at a gain ended up worth just 0.9¢ more, relative to the price at the time, than shares held at a loss; for the largest wallets the difference is zero or reversed. The wallets most prone to the effect earned more per dollar and were more often profitable than the least prone. On a market whose prices partly reverse after moves, selling after a rise is close to the right thing to do.

4 · Risk-taking after wins and losses

Do traders take more risk after winning, playing with "house money" (Thaler and Johnson, 1990), or after losing, trying to get even? Here risk-taking is measured by the share of buying in long-shot contracts under 10¢ and by the average price paid. After any large result, wallets shift slightly toward long shots, more after losses (4–5 points) than after wins (2–3 points). But large wins and losses are not random: they happen to particular kinds of wallets at particular times. The coin-flip markets give the clean comparison. There the gap between unlucky losers and lucky winners shrinks to 0.6 points, at the edge of statistical significance. A weak break-even effect, and no sign of house money.

5 · Long-shot buyers

A small group of wallets buys mostly long shots. Those putting half or more of their money into contracts under 10¢ (average price 10¢) lost 25% of what they spent, and only 27% of them came out ahead. Wallets that rarely buy long shots roughly broke even. The calibration page shows that contracts under 10¢ win about as often as their last traded price says. So the losses are probably not mispricing of long shots as such but how they are bought: a 1¢ spread is a tenth of the price of a 10¢ contract, and taking it again and again in thin markets adds up.

How this was built

Data. Every trade in 74,099 resolved yes/no Polymarket markets (October 2023 to October 2026) from the public Data API, both sides of every fill: 173.9 million records. Activity is measured in markets in this sample, so trading in markets that had not resolved by October 2026 is not counted.

Coin flips. Markets whose last traded price a day before resolution was 40–60¢. A wallet enters at its first such market if it held at least 100 net shares of one outcome at resolution and bought something in the 8 weeks before; events in the last 12 weeks of the data are excluded so that every wallet has a full 12 weeks after.

Records. Quarterly snapshots built only from markets resolved before each quarter began: number of markets, total profit, and a t-statistic of profit per dollar across markets.

Disposition. Average purchase price is the dollar-weighted price of all the wallet's earlier purchases of that outcome in that market. Positions created by splitting a dollar into Yes and No shares are not visible as purchases, so orders by wallets without a recorded purchase are left out.

Limits. Wallets are not people: one trader can run many wallets, and a wallet that stops trading may have moved to another. Returns exclude fees and liquidity rewards.

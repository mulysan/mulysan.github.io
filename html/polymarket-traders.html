Who wins on Polymarket, and whose trades carry information?

Shmuel San · October 2026

Every trade on Polymarket is recorded with the wallet that made it. This page reconstructs the profit and loss of 2.1 million wallets across 74,000 resolved markets, and follows the price after 757,000 large orders to ask which trades carried information and which only moved the price for a while. It is a companion to How much do Polymarket prices know, and when?

2,099,419 wallets173.9m trade records74,099 marketsOct 2023–Oct 2026

What the data show

  1. Most traders lose, and a few win nearly everything. 30% of wallets made money; the median wallet lost about $1. The top 1% of wallets took 94% of all gains ($497m), the top 0.1% took 73%, and the 100 biggest winners alone took 37%. Losses are just as concentrated: the worst 1% of wallets account for 84% of all losses.
  2. Size and role matter more than anything else. Small wallets lose a large share of what they spend (−25% for wallets that traded under $100). Only wallets that traded more than $1m are profitable in aggregate (+$128m, +1.7% of money spent). Wallets that mainly post quotes made $103m; wallets that mainly take quotes lost $83m.
  3. Skill persists, but weakly. Among wallets that spent at least $1,000 in both halves of the sample, 48% of first-half winners profit again, against 28% of first-half losers. The rank correlation of returns across halves is only 0.08.
  4. Large orders carry information, and the market prices it about right. A large order (≥ $1,000) moves the price by 0.7¢ in its direction on average; about a third of that fades within a day. The outcome bears the order out by 0.85¢, so almost nothing is left after the immediate move. Small orders do the opposite: they push the price slightly too far.
  5. Mentions markets look like noise; sports and big one-sided bettors look informed. In "will X say / tweet …" markets, a large order moves the price by 3.1¢, and the move has fully reversed a week later. In sports, large orders know more than the price absorbs. Wallets that take a large one-sided position in a market (≥ 10% of its volume, ≥ 90% in one direction) are strongly informed, in elections too, and the market under-reacts to them by about 2¢.
  6. "Insider-looking" new wallets are mostly not insiders. Large orders from brand-new wallets that trade only one to three markets carry no information on average, and their price impact fades. Documented insider cases exist, but they are a small minority of such wallets.

1 · Profits and losses across traders

For every wallet and market: cash received from sales, minus cash paid for purchases, plus $1 for each share of the winning outcome still held at resolution. This is exact even when shares were created by splitting a dollar into a Yes and a No share, or merged back. Summed over all wallets, profits in each market net to zero within 1% of its volume in 98% of markets, a check that no trades are missing. Fees, liquidity rewards and markets outside the sample are not included, and one person can control several wallets.

2 · Is skill persistent?

Is a profitable wallet just lucky? Markets are split at the median resolution date (August 2025), and wallets are ranked by their return in each half. Return is used rather than total profit, because total profit is driven mainly by how much a wallet bets. The diagonal of the table would be 20% everywhere if past returns said nothing about future ones.

Wallets in the best and worst quintiles tend to end up in either extreme again: these are wallets that make large, risky bets. Past winners are profitable more often (48% against 28%) and earn more in aggregate (+1.7% against −0.6%), but the rank correlation is only 0.08. A small group of skilled traders exists; most of the dispersion is luck.

3 · What happens after a large order

An order is a wallet's purchases or sales in one market at one moment, where the wallet traded against quotes already in the book. For every order of $1,000 or more placed at least an hour before the event at a price between 2¢ and 98¢, the price is followed from just before the order to resolution. All moves are signed so that positive is in the order's direction.

Two numbers separate information from noise. Information is the final payoff minus the price before the order: how right the order was. Left after the move is the payoff minus the price right after the order. If it is positive, the market under-reacted and the order knew more than the price absorbed. If it is negative, the order pushed the price too far, as noise or manipulation would. This is the observational counterpart of the randomized trades in Rhode and Strumpf's field experiment and Camerer's racetrack bets.

4 · Informed traders and would-be manipulators

Three groups stand in for private information and manipulation.

  • New single-market wallets. Wallets that trade three or fewer markets and placed the order within three days of their first trade, the profile in recent studies of insider trading on Polymarket.
  • Dominant one-sided traders. Wallets responsible for at least 10% of a market's taker volume, at least 90% in one direction. This is the profile of the 2012 Intrade "Romney whale" (Rothschild and Sethi), and also of the 2024 "Théo" election bettor, who turned out to be right.
  • Skilled wallets, out of sample. Wallets whose profit per dollar had a t-statistic above 2 in markets resolving before August 2025, evaluated on their orders in later markets.

Dominant one-sided traders are the most informed group: the outcome bears out their orders by 3.8¢ and the market under-reacts by 1.9¢. That holds in elections and politics as well, so large one-sided election bets were mostly informed, not manipulative. The largest such positions were bets of several hundred thousand dollars against long shots that won. New single-market wallets carry no information on average, and in the regressions their price impact reverses more within a day. Out-of-sample skill helps only a little: orders from first-half skilled wallets carry 2.0¢ of information, against 1.9¢ for unremarkable wallets and 1.1¢ for first-half losers.

How this was built

Data. All trades in 74,099 resolved yes/no Polymarket markets with at least $1,000 in volume (excluding auto-generated recurring series) that closed between October 2023 and October 2026, from Polymarket's public Data API. Each fill appears once for the wallet that took liquidity and once for each wallet whose quote it hit, each from that wallet's own perspective: 173.9 million records. A separate query for taker records identifies which side initiated each fill.

Profit. Cash flows plus the value of net shares at resolution, by wallet and market, summed across markets for each wallet. Wallets are Polymarket proxy wallets, not people; wash-trading clusters, estimated at around a quarter of Polymarket volume, appear as many wallets with offsetting profits.

Orders and prices. Prices are those of taker fills, expressed as the price of the first listed outcome. Event time is the earlier of the market's scheduled end and its close. Standard errors are clustered by event, since candidates in one race are mutually exclusive.

Limits. Large orders are not randomly assigned, so their price impact mixes the effect of the order with whatever news prompted it. Group labels such as "dominant one-sided trader" are descriptive. Classifying wallets by outcome would be circular, so none of the groups use the outcome of the market being studied.

Data: Polymarket's public Gamma and Data APIs, retrieved October 2026. Companion page: How much do Polymarket prices know, and when?

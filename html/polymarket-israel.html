What Polymarket's Israeli election markets say, and who is behind the prices

Shmuel San · data as of 2026-10-27 18:26 UTC, election day (polls close 22:00 Israel time)

Polymarket lists about $63m of trading volume on who will be Israel's next prime minister (about $23m of it in orders that took liquidity), and smaller markets on the largest party, Likud's seat count and coalition math. This page, the third of a series, reads those markets with what the full record of 74,000 resolved Polymarket markets teaches (how much prices know; who wins and whose trades carry information): how reliable prices are on election day, and which kinds of traders have tended to know something. Every wallet in the Israel markets is profiled by its behaviour in all other markets. The live section at the top shows prices on Polymarket and Kalshi tonight.

If there is a repeat election: what the gap between the venues implies

Kalshi's prime-minister market resolves "No one" if new elections are called before a government forms. Polymarket's goes to the next person sworn in as prime minister after the 2026 election (deadline 31 December 2027), which would include whoever wins a repeat election. If the two venues price the same beliefs, Polymarket − Kalshi for a candidate is the chance of a repeat election times the chance the candidate wins it, so dividing by Kalshi's "No one" price gives the chance of becoming prime minister after a repeat election. A reading, not a market price: it assumes Polymarket applies its rule this way and ignores the two months' difference in deadlines.

What the data show

  1. The markets favour Gadi Eizenkot and Yashar, more than a week ago. On election evening Eizenkot is at 63¢ to be the next prime minister (49¢ a week ago), Benjamin Netanyahu at 24¢ (28¢), Naftali Bennett at 9¢ (16¢) and Avigdor Lieberman at 2¢. For largest party, Yashar is at 74¢ and Likud at 27¢. The chance of Likud winning fewer than 25 seats is now about 83%. On Kalshi, which counts only a government formed after this election, Netanyahu is lower still (see the live section): its "no government" contract has risen to about 40% today.
  2. Record turnout moved the markets; the experienced money kept selling Netanyahu. After turnout was reported at a record 44.9% at 14:00, Netanyahu fell from 27¢ to 21¢ within an hour. Through the day, wallets with a track record elsewhere sold $370k of Netanyahu on balance, while wallets with no record outside Israel bought $359k, the same split as in the months before. The day's largest trade came from a brand-new wallet that sold $388k of Netanyahu in 16 minutes at 17:31; the price dropped to 19¢ and was back at 23–24¢ within an hour (section 2).
  3. On the last day, election prices have been well calibrated. In 9,621 resolved election markets, contracts priced near today's Israel prices in their last day won at about their price (section 3): 26% for contracts near 24¢, 64% near 63¢, 9% near 9¢, 29% near 27¢ and 74% near 74¢. On that record the prices can be read as probabilities, with an error of a few points either way.
  4. The experienced money is positioned against Netanyahu; the new money is for him. Grouping wallets by their record in every other market, proven forecasters, wallets whose past large orders beat the price, and market makers would all lose if Netanyahu wins and gain if Eizenkot wins. Wallets with a short record, or none outside Israel, hold the mirror-image book: together they gain about $3.3m if Netanyahu wins, up from $1.4m a week ago. The gradient is steep: wallets that made more than $100k elsewhere are positioned $3.3m toward Eizenkot over Netanyahu, and only 19% of them lean Netanyahu, against 67% of wallets with no record outside Israel (figure). Likud's seat market tells the same story: experienced books profit if Likud wins fewer than 25 seats, while short-record and no-record wallets are on 30–34. In the largest-party market every experienced group leans Yashar and wallets with no record outside Israel lean Likud. It is not a by-product of Netanyahu's falling price: in the 11 weeks his price rose, experienced wallets still sold him in 10 (figure). In past markets, the side experienced money traded this steadily won about as often as its price implied, not more (figure). This says who moved the price, not that it will move further: across 27,000 markets a week before resolution, knowing which side experienced wallets hold did not improve on the price (traders page, section 7), and a model that uses the wallets' positions puts Netanyahu at 24–26¢, about where the price is.
  5. Large buys of Netanyahu have mostly reversed. Over the last month, 17 large purchases of Netanyahu ($20k–$100k each, $649k in all) were made at 19¢–37¢; 72% of the money came from wallets with little or no record. Fourteen of them were followed by reversal. In the full universe, orders from such wallets carried no information on average and their price impact faded (traders page), which is what happened here (section 6).
  6. Much of the volume in long-shot candidates is not a view. Markets on candidates priced under 1¢ (Barkat, Katz, Golan, Sa'ar, Cohen) each show $310k–$770k of trading among 950–3,400 wallets, and of the trades that can be matched to a counterparty, 53–91% are between just five pairs of wallets. That is the signature of wash trading or reward farming, not information (section 7). In the three leading candidates' markets, the five busiest pairs account for only 8–17%.
  7. The markets now link Yashar's result and Eizenkot's premiership, and only loosely Likud's and Netanyahu's. Judging by how the prices move together, the chance of Likud being the largest party and Netanyahu becoming prime minister is about 10%, against 6.5% if the two were unrelated and at most 24%. If Likud comes first, Netanyahu becomes prime minister with a 37% chance; if not, 19%. A week ago the Yashar and Eizenkot markets moved independently; over the last week they moved together (correlation 0.28), so the markets now see Eizenkot's premiership as tied to his party finishing first (section 9).
  8. No sign that locals know more. Wallets whose trading hours place them on Israel's clock supply more of the Israel markets' volume than of trading elsewhere, but five wallets account for most of it; without them the share is ordinary. They earned no more in past Israel-related markets than elsewhere, and lean the same way as everyone else today (section 10).
  9. One election-day push, no sustained campaign. In the main markets, large orders that moved the price and then faded, or were pushed again after fading, are 11% of large-order dollars, against 9% in other election markets of the same size. The difference is mostly one wallet: the new wallet that sold $388k of Netanyahu on election day pushed three times as the price recovered after each push. No other wallet re-pushed the same market more than twice (section 8).

1 · The markets now

The prime minister market is one market per candidate; each Yes share pays $1 if that candidate becomes prime minister, so the prices across candidates add up to about 100¢. The largest-party market works the same way. Over the spring and summer, Eizenkot rose from about 10–15¢ to about 50¢, largely at Bennett's expense (Bennett fell from 37¢ in May to 6–15¢). Netanyahu drifted down from about 40¢ to 27–35¢, with a rise to 35–37¢ in the first ten days of October that reversed. In the final week Eizenkot gained another 14 points, to 63¢, mostly from Bennett and in the last two days. These prices are the last trade in the data; the live section above shows the current ones.

2 · Election day: who traded the news

Exit polls cannot be published before polls close at 22:00, so the only hard news during the day was turnout. The Central Elections Committee reported 16.7% at 10:00, 31.5% at noon and 44.9% at 14:00, the highest for that hour since 1973 (38.9% in 2022), then 55.7% at 16:00. Markets read the record as bad news for Netanyahu: on Polymarket his price fell from 27¢ to 21¢ between 14:30 and 15:30, and on Kalshi from about 23¢ to 15¢, while Kalshi's turnout contracts jumped.

Who traded it? The pattern of the past months held. Wallets with a track record elsewhere sold Netanyahu in almost every half hour of the day, $262k before 14:30 and $108k after. Wallets with no record outside Israel bought him on balance, $173k before 14:30 and $186k after, buying the dip. Short-record wallets were roughly flat. Altogether 2,388 wallets traded $2.1m in the Netanyahu market on election day.

The day's largest trade did not fit the pattern. At 17:31 a wallet with no trading history anywhere on Polymarket bought $388k of "No" on Netanyahu in seven orders over 16 minutes, at 79¢–83¢. Each order pushed the price down, to a low of 19¢; within an hour other traders, mostly other no-record wallets, had bought it back to 23–24¢. Whether this was a bet, a hedge of a position elsewhere, or an attempt to move the price on election night, the market did not follow it.

3 · How far to trust prices on election day

The companion page on calibration shows that Polymarket prices are close to calibrated at short horizons, and that election markets are among the best informed. Applied to the Israel contracts, the historical record of election markets in their last day before resolution, at similar prices, says the current prices are about right on average. That does not say whether this election is an exception; it says that, historically, a 63¢ election contract on the last day has won about 64% of the time.

4 · Who is trading: the wallets' record in every other market

Of the 60,132 wallets that traded the Israel markets, 90% also traded at least one of the 74,000 resolved markets in our data, so their behaviour elsewhere can be measured. As a group they are more experienced and more successful than the typical Polymarket wallet: 37% made money overall, against 30% of all wallets; 80% have traded election markets elsewhere and 66% other Israel-related markets (such as Gaza, Iran or Netanyahu markets).

The groups below sort them by that record. The lessons from the full universe say which groups to weight: wallets with a proven, persistent edge and wallets whose large orders beat the price carried information; new and short-record wallets did not, and their large orders tended to reverse (skill persistence, wallet types).

5 · Where each group's money is

In a market with several candidates, a wallet's position in any single candidate is misleading: a market maker who sells "Yes" on every candidate is not betting against all of them. What a book really bets on is visible in its profit under each possible result, because exactly one candidate wins. For each group, the table shows the total profit or loss of its wallets' current positions under each outcome, including what they have already paid and received.

Two caveats. Market makers' books partly reflect the other side of what retail traders bought from them, not a view of their own. And the table shows direction, not how strongly a group disagrees with the price: a group positioned against Netanyahu thinks his chances were below the price at which it traded.

Netanyahu started the year as the favourite, at 52–64¢, and has fallen to 24¢. Is the experienced money against him simply because his price has been falling, so that traders who sold early look smart? If so, the experienced wallets' trading would track the trend: buying him in weeks he rose and selling in weeks he fell. They did not. In the 11 weeks since January in which Netanyahu's price rose, wallets that made more than $10k elsewhere were net sellers of Netanyahu against Eizenkot in 10, on average 24% of the week's volume; in the 22 weeks his price fell they sold in 17, on average 14%. They sold into his rallies even more than into his declines, and their weekly trading is uncorrelated with the price change (−0.09). Wallets with no record outside Israel did the mirror image in both kinds of week. The wallets in between are the ones that followed the trend, buying Netanyahu in weeks he rose. Across all Polymarket markets, groups' weekly trading tends to move slightly with the price (correlations of about +0.1), so the experienced wallets' steady selling here stands out.

The big fall in Netanyahu's price, from about 60¢ to 40¢ in March, came before these wallets held any position worth mentioning. Their position was built gradually, mostly from August onward as volume grew, when his price was already around 30–35¢. It reflects a consistent view that his price was too high, held through his rallies, not a reaction to his decline.

How to read this. Two questions are easy to mix up. The first is whether the experienced wallets have a steady view: they do, and it is not a reaction to the trend. The second is whether that view is more right than the price. Their selling is one of the things that set the price: Netanyahu is at 24¢ partly because they kept selling to anyone who would buy. For their position to be a signal on top of the price, markets would have to stop short of where experienced money wants them, and the side it trades would win more often than its price implies.

That can be checked in the past. In 3,617 resolved markets, the net trading of wallets that had already made more than $10k elsewhere was measured over the 8 weeks before the week-out point, as here. The side they traded toward was usually the favourite, and it won more often than not: 66% of the time in the 636 markets where they traded it consistently. But its price a week out was already 65%. In election markets the consistent side won 60% against a price of 61%. In no group of markets, however strongly or consistently the experienced money traded, did its side beat its price by a margin that could be told apart from chance. In the Netanyahu market today, the side they hold, "not Netanyahu," is priced at 76%; on this record, that is about how often it should be expected to win.

6 · Large orders in the last 30 days

In the full universe (see traders page, section 5), about 38% of large orders are followed by momentum (the price keeps moving their way) and 35% by reversal. Reversal was more common after orders from new and short-record wallets, and momentum after orders from dominant one-sided traders. In the Netanyahu market over the last month, $1.75m of large orders have so far been followed by reversal and $1.06m by momentum. The reversals are concentrated in purchases of Netanyahu, about 70% of them by wallets with little or no record, and in the election-day sales described in section 2.

7 · Trading that carries no view

Polymarket pays liquidity rewards, and an estimated quarter of its volume is wash trading: accounts trading mainly with each other (Sirolly, Ma, Kanoria and Sethi, 2025). In the candidate markets below, the share of all matched volume traded between the five most active pairs of wallets is a simple indicator. It is very high in the long-shot candidates' markets, so their volume figures say nothing about interest in those candidates.

8 · Is anyone pushing the price?

Can manipulation be told apart from honest trading? Not trade by trade: someone who wants the price rather than the payoff leaves the same trace as an overconfident bettor. They pay to move the price, the move fades, and then they push again or sell into the move. The traders page sorts all 757,000 large orders on Polymarket by this trace and checks how they turned out. Orders whose move faded were on the losing side by 4–6¢, while orders whose move held were on the right side by 8.5¢. Sustained price support, meaning the same wallet pushing the same market again after a fade three or more times, accounts for about 1% of large-order dollars.

Applied to the Israel markets, the trace gives a measure that can be compared with other markets before the result is known. Large orders in the main markets break down much like those in other election markets of the same size. The share that faded, with or without another push, is 11% of large-order dollars against 9%, and the difference is mostly the single election-day wallet. The orders that were pushed again after a fade total $547k. Most of it is one wallet on election day: a wallet with no record anywhere that sold $388k of Netanyahu, pushing again three times as the price recovered (section 2). The rest is small: $169k of Netanyahu purchases by 19 wallets, mostly with a short record, which reversed (section 6). No other wallet pushed the same market again after a fade more than twice.

This does not rule out manipulation that the trade log cannot see. Trades through several unrelated wallets, or patient buying that never moves the price by 1¢, would not show up here. Nor does it cover attempts to move the outcome itself rather than the price.

9 · Likud and Netanyahu together

The prime-minister market and the largest-party market each give one probability, but voters and coalition talks link them. What matters is the chance of each combination: Likud first and Netanyahu prime minister, Likud first but someone else in office, and so on. The two prices allow any value from 0% to 24% for "Likud first and Netanyahu prime minister". How the two markets move together narrows it. That method predicts combinations better than assuming independence across resolved news-driven markets on Polymarket (calibration page, section 7).

Over the last week, hourly moves in the two markets were modestly correlated (0.19). That implies about a 10% chance of both, against 6.5% if unrelated. Put as conditional chances: if Likud is the largest party, Netanyahu becomes prime minister with probability 37%; if it is not, 19%. Over the last month as a whole the correlation was weaker (0.13, about 9% for both). In resolved markets the method tends to understate correlation, so the true joint belief is probably somewhat higher, but well below the 24% maximum. The other pair changed more. A week ago the markets for Yashar being the largest party and Eizenkot becoming prime minister barely moved together (correlation −0.04); over the last week they did (0.28), which implies a 53% chance of both against 47% if unrelated. As Eizenkot's price rose, the markets came to treat his premiership as tied to Yashar finishing first.

A book of separate Yes and No contracts cannot bet on the link itself: its payoff is just the sum of what each contract pays. What a view about the link changes is risk. A trader who expects Likud and Netanyahu to rise and fall together can hedge one with the other by holding opposite sides. Few do. Of the 518 wallets with real positions in both markets, almost all hold them in the same direction; only 5% of their dollars sit in hedged books. Across the four outcomes, the groups split as before. Proven forecasters, wallets whose past large orders beat the price, and market makers gain if Netanyahu does not become prime minister, whether or not Likud comes first. Short-record wallets and wallets with no record outside Israel gain if he does, whether or not Likud comes first.

10 · Are locals trading, and do they know more?

Every trade carries a timestamp, so each wallet's daily rhythm hints at where its owner lives: the hours when it never trades are its night. That makes it possible to ask questions that stock-market data cannot answer: do people near an event trade on it, and do they know more?

Two limits matter. A wallet can be placed only if it trades often and has a clear night. About a third of wallets with at least 40 orders do (the rest trade round the clock, like bots and market makers, or too irregularly), and in the Israel markets they account for about a fifth of the dollar volume. And a time zone is not a country: Israel's clock is shared with Eastern Europe, Turkey and western Russia. An attempt to pick out Israelis by their week also failed. Wallets with an Israeli weekend (Friday and Saturday different from Sunday to Thursday) are no more common on Israel's clock than elsewhere. The same goes for wallets that never trade from Friday evening to Saturday evening: about 5% on Israel's clock, about the same as in other time zones.

With those limits, three results. First, among the wallets that can be placed, those on Israel's clock supply 26% of the Israel markets' dollar volume, against their 16% share of all Polymarket orders. But five wallets account for most of it, and the two largest end with no net position after trading long-shot candidates back and forth: the wash-trading pattern of section 7. Leaving out the five largest, Israel's time band supplies 9% of the volume, no more than its share of trading elsewhere. Second, there is no sign of a local edge in the past. In 1,292 resolved Israel-related markets, wallets on Israel's clock earned 1.3% per dollar (95% interval −4% to +9%), less than they earned elsewhere. Their large orders beat the price by 1.6¢ there, against 3.3¢ in other markets, and wallets in the Americas did at least as well in Israel-related markets. Third, today they are positioned like everyone else. In the prime-minister market, 37% of the placed wallets on Israel's clock lean toward Netanyahu over Eizenkot (31% in the Americas), and over the last month they traded neither way on balance.

How this was built

Data. Every trade in the 50 Israel election markets with any volume (the nine events followed since October), from Polymarket's public Data API (both sides of every fill), as of the date above. The record of each wallet elsewhere comes from all trades in 74,099 resolved Polymarket markets, October 2023 to October 2026.

Groups. A wallet's record: markets traded, total profit and return, a t-statistic of its profit per dollar across markets, the share of its trades that took liquidity, its profit in election and Israel-related markets, and the outcome of its past orders of $1,000 or more. Groups are assigned in this order: no record outside Israel; market maker (at least 20 markets, under 20% of fills taking liquidity); proven forecaster (at least 20 markets, t-statistic above 2, positive return); informed large orders (at least 10 large orders whose outcome beat the post-order price by more than 2¢ on average); profitable; unprofitable; short record (under 20 markets).

Positions. Shares bought minus sold, by outcome, valued at $1 if that outcome happens. In multi-candidate events this is exact for the event as a whole even when shares were converted between candidates.

Push patterns. A large order (≥ $1,000) moved the price if the next trade was at least 1¢ further in its direction; the move faded if no more than a quarter was left a day later; it was pushed again if the same wallet's next same-direction order in that market, within a week, came after the fade; it was sold back if the wallet unwound at least half of the position within a day.

Limits. Wallets are not people; one trader can use many wallets, and a group's book can mix views with hedges and market-making inventory. Past records describe behaviour, not certain skill. Prices and positions change quickly on election night: the static sections use trades up to the time above, the live section current prices. Nothing here is investment advice.

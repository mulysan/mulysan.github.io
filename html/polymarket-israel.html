What Polymarket's Israeli election markets say, and who is behind the prices

Shmuel San · data as of 2026-10-19 18:50 UTC, 7 days before the October 27 election

Polymarket lists about $49m of trading volume on who will be Israel's next prime minister (about $18m of it in orders that took liquidity), and smaller markets on the largest party, Likud's seat count and coalition math. This page, the third of a series, reads those markets with what the full record of 74,000 resolved Polymarket markets teaches (how much prices know; who wins and whose trades carry information): how reliable prices are a week before an election, and which kinds of traders have tended to know something. Every wallet in the Israel markets is profiled by its behaviour in all other markets.

What the data show

  1. The markets now favour Gadi Eizenkot and Yashar. Eizenkot is at 51¢ to be the next prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu at 27¢, Naftali Bennett at 13¢ and Avigdor Lieberman at 3¢. For largest party, Yashar is at 72¢ and Likud at 28¢. Prices have swung: Likud briefly drew level in early October (Yashar 49¢, Likud 51¢ a week ago) before Yashar regained and widened its September lead, and the chance of Likud winning fewer than 20 seats jumped from 14¢ to 40¢.
  2. A week out, election prices have been well calibrated. In 8,631 resolved election markets, contracts priced near today's Israel prices 6–10 days before the event won at about their price (section 2): 27% for contracts near 27¢, 48% near 51¢, 14% near 13¢ and 73% near 72¢. On that record the prices can be read as probabilities, with an error of a few points either way.
  3. The experienced money is positioned against Netanyahu; the new money is for him. Grouping wallets by their record in every other market, proven forecasters, wallets whose past large orders beat the price, and market makers would all lose if Netanyahu wins and gain if Eizenkot wins. Wallets with a short record, or none outside Israel, hold the mirror-image book: together they gain about $1.4m if Netanyahu wins. The gradient is steep: wallets that made more than $100k elsewhere are positioned $1.6m toward Eizenkot over Netanyahu, and only 28% of them lean Netanyahu, against 66% of wallets with no record outside Israel (figure). Likud's seat market tells the same story: experienced books profit if Likud wins fewer than 25 seats, while short-record and no-record wallets are on 30–34. The largest-party market shows a weaker version: wallets that made over $10k elsewhere, proven forecasters and market makers lean Yashar and wallets with no record outside Israel lean Likud, but the broad "profitable, no proven edge" group leans Likud. This says who moved the price, not that it will move further: their selling is a large part of why Netanyahu is at 27¢ rather than 35¢. Across 27,000 markets a week before resolution, knowing which side experienced wallets hold did not improve on the price (traders page, section 7), and here proven forecasters' remaining net tilt against Netanyahu is under 4% of all positions.
  4. Large buys of Netanyahu have mostly reversed. Over the last month, many of the biggest orders in the Netanyahu market were purchases of $15k–$100k at 29¢–37¢, about 60% of them by wallets with a short record or none outside Israel. The price has since fallen to 27¢. In the full universe, orders from such wallets carried no information on average and their price impact faded (traders page), which is what happened here (section 5).
  5. Much of the volume in long-shot candidates is not a view. Markets on candidates priced under 1¢ (Barkat, Katz, Golan, Sa'ar, Cohen) each show $290k–$760k of trading among 900–3,400 wallets, and of the trades that can be matched to a counterparty, 53–92% are between just five pairs of wallets. That is the signature of wash trading or reward farming, not information (section 6). In the three leading candidates' markets, the five busiest pairs account for only 8–20%.
  6. No sign of a campaign to hold the price up. In the main candidate markets, the mix of large orders that moved the price and then faded, or were pushed again after fading, is about what other election markets of the same size show (9% of large-order dollars, against 9%). No wallet pushed the same Israel market again after a fade more than twice. The small amount of re-pushing ($119k) is mostly purchases of Netanyahu by wallets with a short record; in past election markets of this size, such re-pushed orders ended up on the losing side by about 6¢ (section 7).

1 · The markets now

The prime minister market is one market per candidate; each Yes share pays $1 if that candidate becomes prime minister, so the prices across candidates add up to about 100¢. The largest-party market works the same way. Over the spring and summer, Eizenkot rose from about 10–15¢ to about 50¢, largely at Bennett's expense (Bennett fell from 37¢ in May to 6–15¢). Netanyahu drifted down from about 40¢ to 27–35¢, with a rise to 35–37¢ in the first ten days of October that has since reversed.

2 · How far to trust prices a week out

The companion page on calibration shows that Polymarket prices are close to calibrated at short horizons, and that election markets are the best informed category a week out (Brier skill about 0.6). Applied to the Israel contracts, the historical record of election markets at the same horizon and similar prices says the current prices are about right on average. That does not say whether this election is an exception; it says that, historically, a 51¢ election contract a week out has won about half the time.

3 · Who is trading: the wallets' record in every other market

Of the 51,995 wallets that traded the Israel markets, 95% also traded at least one of the 74,000 resolved markets in our data, so their behaviour elsewhere can be measured. As a group they are more experienced and more successful than the typical Polymarket wallet: 37% made money overall, against 30% of all wallets; 83% have traded election markets elsewhere and 68% other Israel-related markets (such as Gaza, Iran or Netanyahu markets).

The groups below sort them by that record. The lessons from the full universe say which groups to weight: wallets with a proven, persistent edge and wallets whose large orders beat the price carried information; new and short-record wallets did not, and their large orders tended to reverse (skill persistence, wallet types).

4 · Where each group's money is

In a market with several candidates, a wallet's position in any single candidate is misleading: a market maker who sells "Yes" on every candidate is not betting against all of them. What a book really bets on is visible in its profit under each possible result, because exactly one candidate wins. For each group, the table shows the total profit or loss of its wallets' current positions under each outcome, including what they have already paid and received.

Two caveats. Market makers' books partly reflect the other side of what retail traders bought from them, not a view of their own. And the table shows direction, not how strongly a group disagrees with the price: a group positioned against Netanyahu thinks his chances were below the price at which it traded.

In the full universe (see traders page, section 5), about 38% of large orders are followed by momentum (the price keeps moving their way) and 35% by reversal. Reversal was more common after orders from new and short-record wallets, and momentum after orders from dominant one-sided traders. In the Netanyahu market over the last month, $574k of large orders have so far been followed by reversal and $408k by momentum. The reversals are concentrated in purchases of Netanyahu: about 60% of them by wallets with a short record or none outside Israel, the rest mostly by market makers.

6 · Trading that carries no view

Polymarket pays liquidity rewards, and an estimated quarter of its volume is wash trading: accounts trading mainly with each other (Sirolly, Ma, Kanoria and Sethi, 2025). In the candidate markets below, the share of all matched volume traded between the five most active pairs of wallets is a simple indicator. It is very high in the long-shot candidates' markets, so their volume figures say nothing about interest in those candidates.

7 · Is anyone pushing the price?

Can manipulation be told apart from honest trading? Not trade by trade: someone who wants the price rather than the payoff leaves the same trace as an overconfident bettor. They pay to move the price, the move fades, and then they push again or sell into the move. The traders page sorts all 757,000 large orders on Polymarket by this trace and checks how they turned out. Orders whose move faded were on the losing side by 4–6¢, while orders whose move held were on the right side by 8.5¢. Sustained price support, meaning the same wallet pushing the same market again after a fade three or more times, accounts for about 1% of large-order dollars.

Applied to the Israel markets, the trace gives a measure that can be compared with other markets before the result is known. Large orders in the four main candidate markets break down almost exactly like those in other election markets of the same size. A little more of their volume moved the price and held. Less was sold back within a day. The share that faded, with or without another push, is about the same. Nobody ran a sustained campaign. The orders that were pushed again after a fade total $119k, and $76k of that was purchases of Netanyahu by nine wallets; two-thirds of all re-pushed dollars came from wallets with a short record elsewhere. Those buys reversed (section 5).

This does not rule out manipulation that the trade log cannot see. Trades through several unrelated wallets, or patient buying that never moves the price by 1¢, would not show up here. Nor does it cover attempts to move the outcome itself rather than the price.

How this was built

Data. Every trade in the 50 Israel election markets with any volume, from Polymarket's public Data API (both sides of every fill), as of the date above. The record of each wallet elsewhere comes from all trades in 74,099 resolved Polymarket markets, October 2023 to October 2026.

Groups. A wallet's record: markets traded, total profit and return, a t-statistic of its profit per dollar across markets, the share of its trades that took liquidity, its profit in election and Israel-related markets, and the outcome of its past orders of $1,000 or more. Groups are assigned in this order: no record outside Israel; market maker (at least 20 markets, under 20% of fills taking liquidity); proven forecaster (at least 20 markets, t-statistic above 2, positive return); informed large orders (at least 10 large orders whose outcome beat the post-order price by more than 2¢ on average); profitable; unprofitable; short record (under 20 markets).

Positions. Shares bought minus sold, by outcome, valued at $1 if that outcome happens. In multi-candidate events this is exact for the event as a whole even when shares were converted between candidates.

Push patterns. A large order (≥ $1,000) moved the price if the next trade was at least 1¢ further in its direction; the move faded if no more than a quarter was left a day later; it was pushed again if the same wallet's next same-direction order in that market, within a week, came after the fade; it was sold back if the wallet unwound at least half of the position within a day.

Limits. Wallets are not people; one trader can use many wallets, and a group's book can mix views with hedges and market-making inventory. Past records describe behaviour, not certain skill. Prices and positions change quickly before an election. Nothing here is investment advice.
